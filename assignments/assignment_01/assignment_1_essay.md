I am currently a senior majoring in physics with a minor in 
data science. I do not have plans to pursue graduate studies in 
any capacity, but to me, physics is the most interesting 
subject of all time (I’m biased). I work in a particle lab on 
campus, so I would say I am used to hands-on lab work. I have 
always done minimal computing in the lab, but I think I am 
pretty proficient in Python, particularly for data analysis. 
Recently, I was able to get the senior thesis project I wanted, 
and it involves compiling and analyzing a ton of data. One plan 
was to take advantage of the HPC cluster at William & Mary, but 
we found a way around it. Even without the need, hearing about 
it really interested me and pushed me to sign up for the class 
for a structured learning experience rather than picking it up 
on the go.

My motivations for learning high-performance 
computing are honestly not entirely academic or noble. I 
figured that if I was going to take interesting but difficult 
classes at any point, I would save them up for senior year when 
I could take them with lighter course loads. High-performance 
computing is something pretty foreign to me, and I think it 
would be a really strong skill to have no matter what I choose 
to do.

My first goal for the course is to be consistently able 
to navigate GitHub without the desktop app, which is what I am 
used to using. I’ve never actually successfully used the 
command line, so this first assignment was pretty new and 
exciting to me. The syllabus also mentions utilizing large 
datasets, which is a huge part of my thesis, and honestly would 
be part of any physics major’s post-undergraduate job. I am 
used to the datasets used in my lab, which are all done by us 
as undergraduate research assistants, so they are not as large 
as we would wish. I am interested in seeing how we deal with 
data differently than in the core data science classes. Lastly, 
I’ve always been good at following directions through someone 
else’s process/pipeline, but I would be really interested in 
implementing my own.

It personally takes me a while to memorize 
anything, especially things that are basically like shortcuts 
(commands like “mkdir”, “cd”), so I know I need to be spending 
time outside of class/assignments working independently. I do 
not plan on holding myself to a strict schedule of a certain 
number of hours spent practicing mindlessly. I know I will 
learn best by just letting myself play around and mess with 
stuff until I find my own formulas for everything.

I think that the folder structure I created (or rather, 
followed from the assignment) is good for reproducibility 
because I followed the concept of keeping things as simple and 
understandable as possible. Someone without outside context 
should be able ot understand the entire “assignment_01” folder, 
so hopefully it is completely self-contained. I think that the 
folder at face value is pretty digestible, but I admit that my 
process was pretty messy, and I would not expect someone to be 
able to follow my logic and little notes yet. I know that 
organizing my workflow is something I want to work on through 
the semester.

To my understanding, documented code is used for 
computational research tasks with group research in mind. If I 
were to modify something in my lab group’s repository, I should 
document it well enough that a fellow group member could pull 
it and understand it without having to ask me. It is also good 
for personal use (especially when you are a procrastinator) 
when working on a project with lots of components and things 
get messy. Documented code and version history let us “go back 
in time” to a previous version without including a new bug.
